Sounds Confident. Is It Correct?

Generative AI can produce clear and convincing answers, but convincing is not the same as correct. Explore two examples and practice deciding what should be verified.

Core Principle

AI can produce information that is false, invented, incomplete, outdated, insufficiently contextualized, or inappropriate for your specific purpose. AI-generated information can also be technically accurate in one context but dangerously wrong in another. A confident tone is not evidence of accuracy.

Story 1: The Bromide Case

Documented Case:

“In a published medical case, a 60-year-old man reported consulting ChatGPT while conducting a personal experiment to eliminate sodium chloride from his diet. He purchased sodium bromide online and consumed it in place of sodium chloride for approximately three months. He developed bromide toxicity, including serious psychiatric symptoms, and required hospitalization.”

Limits of the Evidence

The treating physicians did not have access to the original ChatGPT conversation. We therefore do not know the user’s exact prompt, the complete AI response, the model used, what context the user provided, or whether the response contained warnings.

What the physicians tested:

Because the original conversation was unavailable, the physicians conducted a separate test. They asked ChatGPT 3.5 what chloride could be replaced with. The response included bromide and stated that context mattered, but it did not provide a specific health warning or ask why the substitution was being considered. This test did not reproduce or verify the patient’s original conversation.

“Technically related does not mean safe, appropriate, or useful.”

Teaching Point: This scenario is better described as a context failure, unsafe omission, or inappropriate application than as a classic hallucination. A hallucination generally involves fabricated or false information. In this case, the proposed substitution may have had meaning within chemistry, but was unsafe when transferred to a dietary and medical context.

Different Failure, Same Responsibility

Comparing these two scenarios highlights how AI risks manifest differently, but require the same fundamental habit of verification.

Story 1: Bromide Case

  • Context: High-consequence personal health decision.
  • Evidence: Original prompt and response unavailable.
  • Nature of AI output: Information may have been technically relevant in chemistry, but dangerous in a dietary context.
  • Core Failure: Missing context, unsafe omission, overreliance, and lack of professional verification.

Story 2: Invented Citation

  • Context: Lower-consequence academic assignment context.
  • Evidence: Source does not exist in any database.
  • Nature of AI output: AI generated realistic-looking bibliographic details from scratch.
  • Core Failure: Clear example of hallucinated (completely fabricated) information.
In both situations, the AI’s confident presentation is not proof. The user remains responsible for checking important information.

Core Principles to Remember:

  • AI output is a starting point, not automatic evidence.
  • Confident wording does not guarantee accuracy.
  • Ask what context may be missing.
  • Verify citations by locating and opening the original source.
  • Increase verification as the possible consequences increase.
  • Consult qualified professionals for health, legal, financial, or safety decisions.
